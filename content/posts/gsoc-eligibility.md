GSoC eligibility is a set of conditions, not a single question such as "Am I a student?" A student can be ineligible because of age, residence, work authorization, prior participation, or a conflicting program role. A working developer can be eligible if they qualify as an open-source beginner and meet the remaining rules.

This guide turns the current official criteria into a decision process. It cannot decide immigration, employment, sanctions, or other legal questions. When your case is unusual, use the official rules and contact path rather than relying on advice from applicants in a chat group.

## The Current Eligibility Requirements

The [official GSoC FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq) lists five contributor requirements. You must:

1. be at least 18 years old at registration;
2. be eligible to work in your country of residence;
3. be a student or an open-source beginner;
4. have been accepted into GSoC no more than once previously; and
5. not reside in a United States embargoed country.

These are joined by **and**. Passing four out of five is not enough, and Google says it does not make eligibility exceptions.

Program rules can change. Country restrictions in particular are time-sensitive. Check the [current FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), [program rules](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/rules), and the GSoC web app for the year in which you apply.

## A Step-by-Step Eligibility Decision Tree

Work through this sequence before choosing an organization.

### Step 1: Will you be 18 at registration?

If you are under 18 at the time required by the program, stop. The FAQ explicitly says that turning 18 after registration does not make you eligible for that cycle.

Do not substitute the proposal deadline, coding start date, or final evaluation date for the official registration point. Use the date and wording shown for the current program.

### Step 2: Are you eligible to work where you reside?

This is about your actual country of residence and your legal ability to participate in paid work or a stipend-supported program there. Citizenship alone may not answer it.

Potential complications include:

- student visa work limits;
- restrictions on paid activity;
- required institutional authorization;
- limits on weekly hours;
- a planned move during the program; and
- tax or residency documentation.

Google's proposal guide tells applicants on student visas or other visas to verify whether restrictions affect their participation. The FAQ tells F-1 students in the United States to consult their school's international student office and says Google cannot provide CPT or OPT authorization or specialized letters.

If you are unsure, ask the qualified authority for your situation. A mentor, past contributor, or blog author cannot grant work authorization.

### Step 3: Are you a student or an open-source beginner?

This is an either-or gateway. You do not have to satisfy both.

If you are a student, verify the official definition and any documentation required in the current rules. Do not assume that a course, boot camp, informal study program, or future admission automatically qualifies without checking.

If you are not relying on student status, evaluate whether you are an open-source beginner. Google says a beginner has minimal open-source experience and gives examples that can still qualify:

- personal, class, or boot-camp projects;
- open-source software used only within one institution;
- a small number, fewer than ten, of issues or pull requests across different open-source packages; and
- continued involvement in a project first joined while preparing for GSoC.

Google also says regular contributors to an open-source project are not beginners. The distinction is about meaningful experience, not merely account age or the number of green squares on a profile.

### Step 4: How many times have you previously been accepted?

The current rule says you must have been accepted into GSoC **no more than once previously**.

That means:

- never accepted before: this condition is satisfied;
- accepted once before: this condition is satisfied;
- accepted two or more times before: this condition is not satisfied.

Count acceptance, not only successful completion. Do not assume that withdrawing or failing erases a previous acceptance. If your history is unusual, confirm it through the official contact path.

### Step 5: Have you served as a mentor or organization administrator?

The FAQ says a person cannot be both a mentor and a contributor and that once someone has been a GSoC mentor or organization administrator, they cannot return as a GSoC contributor.

This is separate from the previous-acceptance count. If you held one of these roles, check the current rule before applying.

### Step 6: Does your residence meet current location rules?

Google's restrictions can reflect United States sanctions and program-specific rules. They can change. The FAQ currently names some ineligible locations and distinguishes them from nearby eligible areas, but copying that list into a permanent checklist risks becoming stale.

Use the current official FAQ and rules. Evaluate where you actually reside during registration and participation, not only nationality or birthplace. If the wording does not clearly cover your case, ask Google through its official contact route.

### Step 7: Can you make the required individual commitment?

Formal eligibility does not guarantee that participation is practical. Proposals must be submitted by individuals, not groups. Accepted contributors need reliable Internet access, regular communication with mentors, and enough time for the agreed project scope.

Google currently describes project sizes of about 90, 175, and 350 hours. Projects can run for 8 to 22 weeks. Inventory classes, exams, employment, caregiving, travel, health needs, and other commitments before proposing a schedule.

An applicant can be legally eligible but unable to meet a particular organization's project requirements. That is a fit problem, not necessarily an eligibility problem.

## How to Evaluate Beginner Status

Beginner status creates the most uncertainty because experience is qualitative. Use an evidence inventory instead of looking for a loophole.

List every open-source activity under these headings:

| Evidence | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Issues and pull requests | How many were substantive? Were they across unrelated packages or sustained in one community? |
| Maintainer responsibility | Did you review others, triage regularly, make releases, or set project direction? |
| Duration | Was this a short preparation period or recurring involvement over a long period? |
| Project reach | Was the software used only in a class or institution, or by a broader public community? |
| Employment | Was your open-source work a regular part of a paid role? What responsibility did you hold? |
| Community role | Were you recognized as a regular contributor, committer, maintainer, mentor, or administrator? |

No unofficial scoring formula can turn this into a binding answer. The inventory helps you describe your experience honestly if the application asks. It also prevents two opposite mistakes: excluding yourself because of three small pull requests, or calling yourself a beginner despite long-term maintainer responsibility.

Do not delete public contributions, minimize a role, or create a new account to appear less experienced. Eligibility statements should match the public record.

## Common Eligibility Scenarios

### University student with no open-source experience

The student-or-beginner condition is likely satisfied through student status, and the person also appears to be a beginner. They must still pass the age, work-eligibility, residence, and prior-participation checks.

### Recent graduate who has never contributed to open source

Current enrollment is not the only route. The person may qualify as an open-source beginner if all other conditions are met.

### Employed developer with closed-source experience

Professional software experience does not by itself equal open-source experience. The person may qualify as a beginner, but should assess time availability and any open-source responsibilities connected to work.

### Developer with eight small pull requests across different libraries

The FAQ gives fewer than ten issues or pull requests across various packages as an example that can still be beginner experience. Substance and continuity still matter, and the remaining requirements still apply.

### Regular contributor to the organization they want to apply to

Google states that regular open-source contributors are not beginners. Student status and the full official rules may still need to be considered, but the person should not assume the beginner route applies.

### Applicant accepted once before

The current "no more than once previously" rule permits someone with one prior acceptance to satisfy that specific condition. It does not waive any other requirement.

### Past GSoC mentor who is now a student

The current FAQ says past mentors or organization administrators cannot return as contributors. Student status does not override that role restriction.

### Applicant who will turn 18 during community bonding

They are not eligible for that cycle if they were under 18 at registration.

## Eligibility Is Different From Organization Requirements

Google's rules determine whether you may participate in the program. Organizations can add application requirements related to their work, such as:

- proficiency in a language or platform;
- a qualification task;
- a contribution before proposal review;
- use of a required proposal template;
- communication in a specified channel;
- attendance at certain meetings;
- agreement to an AI-use policy; or
- familiarity with a domain or development environment.

Failing an organization prerequisite does not necessarily make you ineligible for GSoC. It means that organization or project is not currently a viable application target.

Use the [organization selection workflow](/blog/post/how-to-use-gsoc-organizations-data) to distinguish program eligibility from project fit.

## AI Use Does Not Change Eligibility, but Policy Still Matters

The official FAQ tells applicants to check each organization's policy on AI-generated text and code. Some organizations may automatically reject proposals that violate their guidance.

This is usually an application-compliance issue rather than one of the five headline eligibility conditions. It can still end your candidacy. Read the policy before using AI for proposal text, code, research, translation, or editing. If disclosure is required, provide it. Your proposal must remain truthful and reflect understanding you can demonstrate yourself.

## Documents and Facts to Prepare

Before applications open, record:

- date of birth and the relevant registration date;
- current country of residence;
- basis for work eligibility;
- student status if you rely on it;
- open-source experience inventory if you rely on beginner status;
- prior GSoC applications, acceptances, and outcomes;
- any mentor or organization-administrator role;
- visa or institutional constraints;
- available hours and unavailable dates; and
- the official rule or FAQ link supporting each conclusion.

Accepted participants may need tax forms and proof of residency. Do not upload sensitive documents to public repositories or send them to mentors unless the official process instructs you to do so.

## What to Do If Your Case Is Unclear

1. Read the current FAQ and rules in full.
2. Write your facts without personal identifiers or unnecessary sensitive details.
3. Identify the exact sentence that creates ambiguity.
4. Use the official GSoC contact route for program interpretation.
5. Use a qualified immigration, employment, tax, or institutional adviser for legal authorization questions.
6. Save the response and date because rules can change between cycles.

Do not ask an organization to promise that Google will consider you eligible. Mentors select projects within the program; they do not rewrite program rules.

## Final Eligibility Checklist

- [ ] I will be at least 18 at the required registration point.
- [ ] I am eligible to work in my country of residence.
- [ ] I qualify as a student or an open-source beginner under current rules.
- [ ] I have been accepted into GSoC no more than once before.
- [ ] I have not held a role that prevents me from returning as a contributor.
- [ ] My residence complies with current location restrictions.
- [ ] I can participate as an individual and meet the project's time commitment.
- [ ] I checked the official rules for this program year.
- [ ] I separated Google eligibility from organization-specific prerequisites.
- [ ] I sought official or qualified advice for any unresolved legal or rule question.
