GSoC eligibility is not limited to university students. Under the rules published as of August 12, 2026, an applicant must be at least 18 years old at registration, be a student or an open-source beginner, be eligible to work in the country where they will reside during the program, satisfy current geographic restrictions and have been accepted into GSoC no more than once before. Each condition matters: meeting one does not waive the others.

The most authoritative checks are the official [Get Started eligibility list](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/get-started), [GSoC FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq) and current [Program Rules](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/rules). This guide explains their practical meaning; it cannot decide immigration, employment or tax law for an individual.

:::callout Annual verification required
Eligibility can change between program editions. The statements below describe the public rules verified on August 12, 2026. Reopen the official pages for the cycle in which you apply instead of relying on a saved checklist.
:::

## Current official GSoC eligibility criteria

Use this decision tree before spending weeks on an application:

1. **Will you be at least 18 when you register?** If no, you are not eligible for that cycle.
2. **Are you a student or an open-source beginner?** At least one route must apply. If your beginner status is unclear, assess the substance of your experience rather than one count.
3. **Are you legally eligible to work in the country where you will reside during the program?** If no or uncertain, resolve that with the appropriate authority or qualified adviser.
4. **Does your residence comply with the current geographic rules?** Check the live Program Rules and FAQ because restrictions are volatile.
5. **Have you been accepted into GSoC no more than once previously?** If you already exceeded the current prior-acceptance limit, you are not eligible as a contributor.
6. **Have you previously served as a GSoC mentor or organization administrator?** The current FAQ says a person who has served in either role cannot later return as a contributor.
7. **Are you applying as an individual and not simultaneously as a mentor?** Team proposals are not allowed, and one person cannot be a mentor and contributor in the same program.

Passing this tree establishes formal eligibility only. An organization must still decide that your project, skills, communication and plan fit its needs and available mentorship. Read [what GSoC is and how the roles work](/blog/post/what-is-gsoc) before interpreting eligibility as selection.

## The GSoC age rule

The current minimum age is straightforward: you must be **18 or older at the time of registration**. The official FAQ explicitly says that turning 18 after registration does not make you eligible for the current cycle, and Google says it does not make exceptions to the formal requirements.

The relevant event is registration, not the first day of coding, the proposal deadline, the results announcement or your birthday later in the year. If a future cycle changes the registration flow or wording, follow that year's terms.

Someone younger than 18 can still use the year productively. They can learn Git, build projects, read open-source contribution guides and participate in communities that permit minors under their own policies. That activity does not create an age exception, but it can build genuine readiness for a later cycle. The [preparation roadmap](/blog/post/gsoc-preparation-roadmap) is designed around evidence you can produce without pretending an application is already available.

## Students and non-students

GSoC began as a student-focused program, but the Contributor Guide records that the program expanded in 2022 to include students **and** other open-source beginners aged 18 or older. Current eligibility therefore uses an either/or gateway:

- a person may qualify through current student status; or
- a person who is not a student may qualify as an open-source beginner.

This does not mean every adult non-student is eligible. A non-student with extensive, regular open-source experience may fail the beginner requirement. It also does not mean every student is automatically selectable: the student must satisfy age, residence, work and prior-participation rules, and then meet the organization's technical expectations.

Community or junior-college students are eligible under the current FAQ. Someone who graduates during the program may also participate under the current FAQ. Those examples clarify that the program does not require one narrow university path, but applicants should still represent their current status accurately.

## What counts as an open-source beginner?

The official FAQ describes a beginner as someone with **minimal open-source experience** and says regular contributors are not beginners. It provides examples of experience that can still be compatible with beginner status:

- personal, classroom or boot-camp projects;
- open-source software used only within one institution, such as a club site or university research project;
- a small number - described as fewer than ten - of issues or pull requests scattered across different open-source packages;
- continued work in a project first joined as part of GSoC preparation.

These examples require context. They are not a points system and do not promise eligibility at a specific count. Ten trivial patches do not establish the same history as years of maintaining releases; one sustained, high-responsibility role may be more significant than many isolated issue comments. Likewise, making real contributions while preparing does not automatically disqualify someone: the FAQ expressly recognizes continued involvement in a project joined for preparation.

A responsible self-audit asks:

- Have I held an ongoing maintainer, reviewer or release role in a public open-source project?
- Do communities already rely on me as a regular contributor rather than a newcomer?
- Is most of my experience coursework, personal work, isolated fixes or recent preparation?
- Can I describe dates, repositories and responsibilities accurately if asked?

Do not hide or minimize experience to fit a label. If the answer remains ambiguous, consult the current official terms and GSoC support rather than relying on a community vote.

## Graduates and working professionals

A completed degree does not itself make someone eligible or ineligible. Student status is no longer the only route, so a graduate may apply if they are an open-source beginner and meet every other rule. A professional job title also does not decide the result: a software engineer may have minimal public open-source experience, while someone without a software title may already be a long-term maintainer.

The current FAQ notes that GSoC targets newcomers and that professionals often find the time commitment difficult to balance. That is a feasibility warning, separate from the formal rule. Before applying, a working professional should document:

- contracted work hours and on-call duties;
- employer policies concerning outside work and intellectual property;
- leave, travel and time-zone constraints;
- the proposed GSoC scope and meeting expectations;
- all outside commitments that the proposal should disclose.

The official proposal guide asks applicants to inventory outside commitments and discuss known absences early. Formal eligibility without realistic availability can still lead to a weak or unworkable application. Use the [application guide](/blog/post/how-to-apply-for-gsoc) to turn availability into a dated artifact instead of a vague promise.

## Previous contributors, mentors and organization administrators

Prior roles need careful separation:

| Prior history | Current practical reading | Verification needed |
|---|---|---|
| Applied but was not accepted | Not a prior acceptance | Recheck all other eligibility rules |
| Accepted once previously | The FAQ's "no more than once previously" condition can still be satisfied | Confirm the current cycle's rules and accurately disclose history |
| Accepted more than once previously | Does not satisfy the current stated contributor condition | Read current terms; do not assume another contributor application is allowed |
| Served as a GSoC mentor | Current FAQ says a mentor cannot later return as a contributor | Consider mentor or community roles instead |
| Served as a GSoC organization administrator | Current FAQ applies the same contributor restriction | Verify the current wording before choosing a role |
| Wants to mentor and apply in one cycle | Not allowed | Choose one role |

This table is a plain-language map, not a substitute for the Program Rules. Program history can include different policies, and account records may matter. If you have a complicated prior role, use official support rather than omitting it.

## Country, residence and work eligibility

Current contributor eligibility requires you to be legally eligible to work in your country of residence during the program and to reside in a country permitted by current geographic rules. Residence is not a decorative profile field: it also informs the stipend table and payment process.

As of August 12, 2026, the official FAQ lists participants in Russia, Belarus and the DNR/LNR regions as ineligible, while stating that people elsewhere in Ukraine are not subject to that specific restriction. The broader requirement is residence outside countries currently embargoed by the United States. Because sanctions and program terms can change, do not copy this list into a future application without reopening the official pages.

Work authorization is a separate legal question. The FAQ directs F-1 students in the United States to their institution's international student office and says Google cannot provide CPT/OPT authorization or specialized letters. A visa, school rule, employer restriction or local classification may affect an individual even when the general GSoC page looks permissive.

If you may live in more than one country during the coding period, resolve the plan before relying on a stipend figure. The [GSoC stipend guide](/blog/post/gsoc-stipend) explains the 2026 country-of-residence rule without pretending to provide immigration or tax advice.

## Eligibility examples: fact versus interpretation

| Situation | What the official material establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| A 19-year-old university student with class projects | The age and student gateway may be satisfied | Work eligibility, residence compliance or organization selection |
| A graduate with two isolated documentation PRs | Non-students can qualify through beginner status; minimal scattered work can be compatible | Automatic beginner status or acceptance |
| A full-time engineer with no public contributions | A professional title is not an automatic ban | That private experience is irrelevant to project fit or available time |
| A recent newcomer who keeps contributing to one project during preparation | Continued involvement after joining for preparation can remain compatible with the beginner example | Unlimited contribution history without any reassessment |
| A 17-year-old who turns 18 during coding | The current age rule is not satisfied at registration | An exception based on later age |
| A person already accepted twice | The current prior-acceptance condition is not satisfied | A waiver based on a new organization |
| A former GSoC mentor | Current FAQ blocks returning as a contributor | A restriction on continuing to help as a mentor, subject to mentor rules |

The discipline here is to separate a **fact** from a **conclusion**. An example can show that one condition may be compatible; it rarely proves that the whole application is eligible.

## Documents and verification

At application time, answer every representation truthfully and keep supporting information consistent. Accepted contributors must later provide proof of residency and any required tax forms, according to the official FAQ and [accepted-contributor information](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/accepted-students). Payment-provider identity checks are not a substitute for checking eligibility before applying.

A useful verification folder can contain:

- a saved link to the current Program Rules and contributor terms;
- the exact registration and proposal deadlines in UTC;
- your residence plan for the coding period;
- a written time inventory and known absences;
- an accurate list of prior GSoC roles and acceptances;
- a factual summary of open-source repositories and responsibilities;
- questions that require official or professional clarification.

Do not upload sensitive documents to public repositories or community channels. Follow the secure process communicated by the official program and payment provider after acceptance.

## Annual eligibility update checklist

Run this checklist when each new cycle opens:

- [ ] Read the live Get Started page, FAQ, Program Rules and contributor terms.
- [ ] Confirm the age trigger and registration date.
- [ ] Confirm the exact student-or-beginner language and current beginner examples.
- [ ] Recheck the limit on previous acceptances and restrictions on former mentors or administrators.
- [ ] Recheck residence, embargo and location-specific exclusions.
- [ ] Resolve local work-authorization questions with a qualified source.
- [ ] Confirm that your residence during coding matches the information used for program and payment setup.
- [ ] Review organization-specific prerequisites separately; do not confuse them with Google-wide eligibility.
- [ ] Save the date and URL of every rule you rely on.

Eligibility is a gate, not a ranking advantage. Once it is genuinely resolved, the next useful work is to build evidence, choose a suitable community and follow the official process. The [GSoC 2027 guide](/blog/post/gsoc-2027-guide) tracks what has and has not been announced, while the [preparation roadmap](/blog/post/gsoc-preparation-roadmap) helps turn an eligible but unprepared starting point into reviewable work.
