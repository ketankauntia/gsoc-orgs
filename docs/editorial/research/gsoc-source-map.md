# Authoritative GSoC Source and Fact Map

Verified **2026-08-12**. Use this map before writing or refreshing any GSoC article.

## Freshness labels

- **Durable:** a program concept that usually persists, but should still be checked during major yearly refreshes.
- **Annual:** dates, amounts, providers, countries, counts, application limits, and policies that must be rechecked for every edition.
- **Organization-specific:** a rule set by one mentoring organization; never generalize it to the whole program.
- **Derived:** a calculation or normalized local-dataset result that requires a displayed method and boundary.

## Program identity

**Durable:** Google Summer of Code is a global, online program that introduces new contributors to open-source communities. It is not employment, a Google internship, or a recruiting program. Contributors are independent developers who may receive stipends; mentors normally come from the participating open-source community, not Google. No travel is required. The long-term goal is sustainable open-source participation, and an organization is not required to merge every piece of submitted code.

Sources: [official FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), [How GSoC works](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/how-it-works/), and the [Contributor Guide](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/).

## Eligibility

**Annual:** the currently published criteria require an individual to be at least 18 at registration, be a student or open-source beginner, be legally eligible to work in the country of residence, meet current geographic rules, and have been accepted into GSoC no more than once previously. An applicant cannot apply as a team or be both a mentor and contributor. Under the current FAQ, someone who already served as a GSoC mentor or organization administrator cannot later apply as a contributor.

The current FAQ describes a beginner as someone with minimal open-source experience. Compatible examples include personal or class projects, open source used only at one institution, fewer than roughly ten scattered issues/PRs, and continued involvement in a project first joined during GSoC preparation. A professional title does not by itself decide eligibility; current experience and every formal rule still matter.

Sources: [Get Started](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/get-started), [FAQ eligibility](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), and [Program Rules](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/rules).

## Timeline

**Durable pattern:** organization applications generally occur in January, accepted organizations appear in February, contributor research and proposals occur in March, reviews happen in April, accepted projects and community bonding begin around May, most coding begins in late May or June, most projects finish around August, and the longest schedules can finish in November.

**2026 only:** organization applications ran January 19–February 3; organizations were announced February 19; proposals ran March 16–31; accepted projects were announced April 30; community bonding ran May 1–24; coding began May 25; and the longest projects conclude in November.

No official 2027 timeline or accepted-organization list was found as of 2026-08-12. Estimated preparation calendars must be labeled as estimates and link to the live source.

Sources: [official timeline](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/timeline), [2026 program](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/programs/2026), and [project dates](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/project-dates).

## Project sizes, hours, and duration

**Annual:** the current framework defines small projects at approximately 90 hours, medium at 175 hours, and large at 350 hours. Small projects default to 8 weeks; medium and large projects default to 12. Current scheduling permits an overall 8–22-week range, while small projects cannot extend beyond 12 weeks. Google says about 75% of projects use the standard 12-week schedule.

Hours express scope, not a guaranteed weekly schedule. Mentor and contributor may adjust scope when a project is over- or under-scoped. Never reduce the model to “40 hours per week for three months.”

Sources: [FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), [accepted contributor information](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/accepted-students), [project dates](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/project-dates), and [time management](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/time-management-for-students.html).

## Stipends and payments

**2026 only:** stipends are adjusted by purchasing-power parity using the contributor's country of residence during coding. The published ranges are USD 750–1,650 for small projects, USD 1,500–3,300 for medium, and USD 3,000–6,600 for large. The 2026 India figures are USD 750, 1,500, and 3,000 respectively. Payments are currently split 45% after the first successful evaluation and 55% after the final successful evaluation; nonstandard schedules change dates.

Recheck the amount table, provider, supported locations, installments, deadlines, identity/residency process, fees, and tax documentation every year. Google does not provide personal tax advice; neither should an editorial article.

Sources: [2026 stipend table](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/student-stipends), [payment instructions](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/payoneer), [tax forms](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/tax-forms), and [accepted contributor information](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/accepted-students).

## Choosing organizations and projects

**Durable:** start with interests, software already used, technical ability, goals, and desired community experience. Google recommends researching roughly three to five organizations, then narrowing to one or two. Inspect the mission, current ideas, repositories, contribution process, issue tracker, required skills, communication culture, mentor capacity, recent projects, and whether the community remains appealing beyond the program.

Organization choice and project choice are separate decisions. A project ideas page should reveal outcomes, skills, possible mentors, size, and sufficient detail to evaluate feasibility. An applicant may propose an original idea only when the organization permits it and mentors consider it useful.

Sources: [Choosing an Organization](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/choosing-an-organization), [Finding the Right Project](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/finding-the-right-project), [applicant advice](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/student-advice), and the [current organization directory](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/programs/2026/organizations).

## Contact and pre-application contributions

**Durable:** use the organization's published public channel. Read the ideas page, contributor guide, archives, repository, and past work before asking a researched question. Observe norms, introduce yourself briefly, and discuss a specific area early rather than requesting personal tutoring at the deadline.

There is no universal Google requirement for a merged PR or a fixed number of contributions. Some organizations require a PR, test, challenge, proposal discussion, or template; others do not. The official anti-spam material mentions one to three meaningful contributions as a possible organization screening mechanism, not a universal quota. Quality, relevance, demonstrated understanding, and communication matter more than counts.

Sources: [Making First Contact](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/making-first-contact), [Communication Best Practices](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/communication-best-practices), [applicant advice](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/student-advice), [Am I Good Enough?](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/am-i-good-enough.html), and [spam-proposal guidance](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/resources/downloads/spam_proposals).

## Proposals

**Annual:** proposals must be submitted through the official site. The current rules allow up to three, but only one may be accepted. Google's advice favors one or two strong applications and reports that more than 94% of accepted people in a referenced prior cohort submitted two or fewer. Drafts can be edited before the deadline, deadlines are not extended, organization templates are binding, and the current Contributor Guide instructs applicants to upload PDF proposals.

**Durable quality elements:** a clear problem, benefit, related work, technical approach, required and optional deliverables, milestones, tests, documentation, risks, fallback scope, relevant experience, outside commitments, and communication plan. An old accepted proposal is evidence of one historical decision, not a template guaranteed to work again.

Sources: [Writing a Proposal](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/writing-a-proposal), [FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), and [applicant advice](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/student-advice).

## Selection and mentors

**Durable:** organizations evaluate and rank proposals using their own criteria. A project needs a committed mentor before it can be ranked. Selection is a three-way fit among contributor, project, and mentor. Organizations are advised not to select solely from a polished document: technical capability, interaction, time, scope, communication, mentor availability, and work-style fit all matter. Google then allocates organization slots; an applicant cannot infer a personal probability from global proposal counts.

Sources: [Selecting Contributors and Mentors](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/mentor/selecting-students-and-mentors), [Selecting a Contributor](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/mentor/selecting-a-student.html), [roles and responsibilities](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/responsibilities), and [Working With Your Mentor](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/working-with-your-mentor).

## Community bonding

**Durable:** community bonding is active preparation. Useful work includes learning community practices, setting up the environment, refining milestones and acceptance criteria, agreeing on meetings and reports, recording absences, reading or improving documentation, making preliminary fixes, and establishing time-zone logistics. Exact dates are annual.

Sources: [accepted contributor information](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/accepted-students), [How It Works](https://summerofcode.withgoogle.com/how-it-works/), and [Working With Your Mentor](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/working-with-your-mentor).

## Evaluations, extensions, and final work

**Annual framework:** mentor/contributor evaluations occur around the midpoint and end. Mentors assess progress, code quality, participation, communication, and agreed scope. A failure ends participation and future stipend payments. The final contributor evaluation and final public work-product link are mandatory under the current guide. A pass or fail should not be a surprise because feedback should be continuous.

Current schedule extensions require contributor/mentor agreement and formal organization-administrator action before final submission begins. A verbal agreement is insufficient. Current medium/large schedules can extend through 22 weeks; extensions do not automatically increase project hours or stipend.

The final work product should be a stable, public link that clearly separates completed, merged, unmerged, and remaining work. A report, comprehensive PR, or purpose-built repository README can work; an undifferentiated repository homepage, ZIP file, or generic fork cannot explain authorship adequately.

Sources: [official evaluations](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/evaluations), [Contributor evaluation guide](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/evaluations), [project dates](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/project-dates), [organization-admin tips](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/oa-tips), and [work-product guidelines](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/help/work-product).

## AI policy

**Annual and organization-specific:** there is no universal permission or ban. Each organization may restrict AI-written proposals, generated code, contribution tasks, or all of them. Some automatically reject prohibited AI-written proposals. The human remains fully responsible for understanding, validating, testing, licensing, and defending submitted work.

Sources: [official AI guidance](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/resources/ai_guidance), [FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq), [Writing a Proposal](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/writing-a-proposal), and [spam-proposal guidance](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/resources/downloads/spam_proposals).

## After completion or rejection

**Durable:** participants can maintain unfinished work, stay active, present results, help newcomers, propose future work, and later mentor. Rejected applicants can request feedback politely, continue contributing without program funding, reduce or reshape the idea, or apply again if eligible. GSoC guarantees neither a Google job nor a merge, internship, or maintainer role.

Sources: [Staying Engaged](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/staying-engaged-with-your-community), [Being Turned Down](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/being-turned-down), [Getting Code Committed](https://google.github.io/gsocguides/student/strategies-for-getting-your-code-committed), and [FAQ](https://developers.google.com/open-source/gsoc/faq).

## Local analysis boundary

The current editorial analysis uses finalized repository snapshots for 2016–2025 and excludes incomplete 2026 projects. That window contains **10,951 project records across 504 normalized organization slugs**. Technology aggregates currently include 2026 organization tags despite missing 2026 project records, so their displayed project counts are historical aggregates and must not be described as finalized 2026 project totals. Generated per-technology pages also attach a profile's globally aggregated technology list to every year in which that profile was active. Use the yearly snapshot's `tech_stack` summary for a year-specific organization-tag count; do not infer year-specific usage by intersecting a global tag with `active_years`.

Always state:

- the year window;
- whether data is announcement-day, live, or finalized;
- how organization names/slugs were normalized;
- whether umbrella organizations and sub-organizations are separate;
- what is missing;
- the calculation and denominator.

Do not silently mix announcement counts and later live counts. Google announced 185 organizations in February 2026, while a later contributor announcement referenced 184; attach an “as of” date and source to current counts.

## Misconceptions to block

- GSoC is a Google internship or employment.
- Only university students may apply.
- Everyone over 18 is eligible.
- A past organization is confirmed for 2027.
- 2027 dates or stipends are already official.
- Every applicant needs a fixed number of merged PRs.
- Three proposals triple selection odds.
- A polished PDF alone determines selection.
- Mentors are Google employees.
- Every project is 40 hours a week for three months.
- Acceptance guarantees payment or tax-free income.
- Community bonding is vacation.
- Contributors can extend deadlines themselves.
- Google provides late proposal extensions.
- AI is universally allowed or universally banned.
- Acceptance immediately permits claiming completed-contributor status.
- GSoC guarantees a job at Google.
